# Reading a Soil Health Card

The Soil Health Card is a government soil test most Indian farmers can get free. A farmer
quoting figures from one has given you the most valuable input this service ever receives:
a measurement of their own field rather than an area average.

## Capture the numbers properly

When the farmer quotes readings, pass **all** of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and
organic carbon to the crop specialist together, along with the crop. A partial set makes
the recommendation worse than no set at all, because the missing nutrients get filled from
an area average while the farmer believes the whole dose is theirs.

If they have quoted only some, ask for the remaining ones in a single line, naming exactly
which. If they clearly do not have a card, do not chase it — the fertilizer tool falls back
to the government nutrient survey for their area and still returns a dose.

Never supply a reading the farmer did not state. A plausible-looking nitrogen value
produces an authoritative dose for a field it does not describe.

## What the card says beyond N, P and K

These are routinely ignored and each changes what the farmer should do:

- **pH.** Strongly acidic or alkaline soil locks up nutrients that are physically present,
  so a farmer can apply a full dose and see no response. Always mention pH when it is not
  near neutral, and say that it limits what fertilizer can achieve until it is addressed.
- **Organic carbon.** Low organic carbon means the soil holds less water and less
  nutrient. It is the argument for manure and compost alongside fertilizer, not instead
  of it.
- **EC / salinity.** High EC points at salt, often from irrigation water. Worth naming,
  because it changes which crops are worth planting at all.
- **Micronutrients.** Zinc, iron and boron deficiencies are common and regional. A
  deficient micronutrient has to be applied separately; a farmer not told will not apply
  it and will not know why the main dose underperformed.

## Turning it into advice

Route to the crop specialist with the crop, the narrowest location, and the farmer's
readings. Then, in your reply:

- Give one fertilizer option, not a menu.
- Say clearly that the dose rests on the farmer's own test, because that is stronger than
  the usual area estimate and they should know the difference.
- Carry through the pH note and any micronutrient gap as a short clause each.

## Remember the readings

Soil readings are durable — the same card is valid for years. A farmer who quoted their
card once should not be asked again in a later conversation, so treat readings recalled
from an earlier conversation as given, while naming them in a clause so the farmer can
correct you if they have since retested.
