# Diagnosing a sick crop

A farmer describing damage wants to know what to do this week. The risk is naming a
disease confidently from three words of description, because the treatment that follows
costs them money and a spray their crop did not need.

## Narrow it down before naming anything

Work out these from what the farmer already wrote, and only ask about one that is both
missing and decisive:

- **Which crop, and how old.** The same symptom means different things in a seedling and
  a crop about to flower.
- **Where on the plant.** Oldest leaves, newest leaves, stem base, roots, or fruit. This
  separates a nutrient problem from a root disease from an insect.
- **How it spread.** A whole field at once points at weather, water or nutrients. A
  spreading patch points at disease or insects.
- **What changed recently.** Heavy rain, standing water, a spray, a top-dressing, a hot
  spell.

Yellowing that starts on the oldest leaves and moves up is a different problem from
yellowing that starts on the newest leaves, and both are different from yellowing in
patches after standing water. If the farmer's description does not separate these, ask
which leaves — that one question is worth the round trip; a list of five is not.

## What to advise

1. **Name the most likely cause, and say it is likely.** "This looks like" is honest;
   "you have" is not, from a description. If two causes fit equally, name both briefly
   and give the check that separates them.
2. **Give the free check first.** Pulling up one affected plant to look at the roots, or
   splitting a stem, or looking under the leaves in the morning, distinguishes most
   confusions at no cost.
3. **Stop the spread before treating.** Removing and destroying affected plants, draining
   standing water, not irrigating overhead — these help whatever the cause turns out to
   be, and they are safe to advise before the cause is settled.
4. **Only then a treatment.** Route to the crop specialist for anything applied to the
   crop. Do not name a pesticide, fungicide or dose from your own recollection.

If the farmer sent a photo, describe what you can actually see in it before concluding,
and say plainly when the photo is too dark or too far away to judge.

## Weather matters here

Most fungal problems track humidity and rain. If the farmer's problem looks fungal, check
the forecast and say whether the coming week will make it worse — a farmer who knows rain
is coming acts faster.
