# Scheduling irrigation

Both failure modes cost a crop. Under-watering at flowering or grain-filling cuts yield
for the season. Over-watering drowns roots, leaches away the fertilizer the farmer just
paid for, and invites root disease. The schedule depends on three things, and a farmer
usually gives you only one.

## The three inputs

- **The crop and its stage.** Water demand is not flat across a season; it peaks around
  flowering and grain or fruit fill. A farmer at that stage needs a different answer from
  one two weeks after sowing.
- **The soil's water-holding capacity.** Light sandy soil holds little and needs smaller,
  more frequent watering. Heavy clay holds much and needs less often, more deeply, and
  waterlogs if pushed. This comes from the irrigation specialist's soil lookup for their
  location, not from guesswork.
- **The coming week's rain.** Irrigating the day before heavy rain wastes water and
  leaches nutrients. This is the single most actionable thing you can tell a farmer and it
  requires only their location.

## How to answer

Route to the irrigation specialist with the crop, the stage if known, and the narrowest
location you have. Then give the farmer, in this order:

1. **Whether to water now or wait**, and if waiting, what to wait for — a number of days,
   or rain in the forecast.
2. **How much**, in the units the tool returned, with the basis stated ("per acre").
3. **One thing to watch** that tells them the schedule is working or not: the soil at a
   hand's depth, the crop wilting in the afternoon but recovering by morning, water
   standing more than a few hours after irrigating.

If the forecast shows meaningful rain within the window, lead with that. It changes what
they do today.

## The free advice worth repeating

- Water early morning or evening rather than midday, so less evaporates.
- Standing water for more than a few hours is damaging, not generous. Drainage matters as
  much as supply on heavy soil.
- Mulching cuts evaporation and is usually available at no cash cost.

## Method changes the answer

A farmer with drip can be told a frequency and duration; a farmer flooding from a channel
cannot act on that and needs intervals and depth instead. Ask which they have only if the
advice genuinely differs and they have never said — and once they have said, it is durable,
so carry it forward rather than asking each season.
